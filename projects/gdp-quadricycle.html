Quadricycle Wheels & Tyres

Wheel and tyre selection, tyre modelling and a tyre life-cycle review for an L7e quadricycle, designed for a Mobility-as-a-Service fleet running between Cranfield and Milton Keynes.

  1. 330 kgFront design load per wheel: 800 kg laden, 55/45 split, 1.5 load factor
  2. 10.6%Load headroom of the chosen tyre (LI 73, 365 kg), kept deliberately small
  3. ±27–30%Cornering-stiffness uncertainty from two literature methods, carried into the handling analysis
  4. 2.4 kgUnsprung mass saved by choosing 155/65 over 155/70 R13

My role

  • Deputy Engineer, Vehicle Dynamics: in a six-person department within a 19-person project team, oversaw the steering, brakes, suspension and ride-dynamics work within the department, and set agendas, project plans and weekly status updates with the Chief Engineer.
  • Wheels and tyres engineer: owned tyre and wheel selection and gave the brakes, steering, chassis and powertrain teams one consistent set of tyre envelope and rolling-radius figures to work from.
  • Failure mode avoidance: led it across the department, identifying likely problems and mitigations for each subsystem.

From a market search across six retailers I chose a 155/65 R13 73T all-season tyre. All-season suits a shared fleet: no seasonal changeovers, so more uptime. Load index 73 (365 kg) clears the 330 kg requirement by 10.6%. A much higher index would mean a heavier, stiffer carcass, adding unsprung mass and making the ride harsher. The 155/65 also has a lower sidewall than the 155/70 alternative, saving about 0.6 kg per tyre.

Wheel selection

A Pugh matrix compared steel, aluminium and steel-with-cover wheels on weight, cost, repairability, maintenance, durability and carbon footprint. Steel won: it costs less to buy, is easier to repair, and copes better with knocks in fleet use, at the cost of extra unsprung mass. The chosen part is an off-the-shelf 13 × 4.5 ET36, 4 × 100 PCD steel wheel with M12 grade 10.9 bolts, so no tooling cost. Fixing the PCD meant the suspension engineer could start the hub design.

Modelling the tyre without test data

There was no measured force-slip data for this tyre, so a Magic Formula fit wasn't possible. CarMaker could generate a tyre model, but the software itself flagged it as not validated. I used a linear model instead (Fy = Cα α, with Cα = nFz), sweeping the normalised cornering coefficient across the typical passenger-car range at unladen (450 kg), part-laden (650 kg) and fully laden (800 kg) mass.

n = 0.15 ± 0.04 deg−1  ⇒  Cα uncertainty ≈ ±27%

The main lesson was treating cornering stiffness as the output of a method with its own uncertainty, not a single number to look up. A sidewall-geometry regression (Hewson, 2005) gave ±30%, and a distribution across typical passenger cars (Gillespie, 1992) gave ±27%. The two methods aren't interchangeable, and neither captures how stiffness changes with vertical load. So the analysis was limited to primary handling, and the uncertainty band was carried into the ride-dynamics engineer's understeer-gradient work rather than hidden.

Simulation and sustainability

  • CarMaker: set up with the simulation engineer to run M1-style steady-state cornering, split-μ braking, rollover and slalom tests. Because the tyre model wasn't validated, these were used for qualitative safety checks only, not for tyre-force results.
  • Tyre life-cycle assessment: my own chapter of the report, covered in the next section.

Tyre life-cycle assessment

I wrote the report's life-cycle chapter for the chosen 155/65 R13 tyre, from raw materials through use to end of life. The question was where a shared fleet's tyres do most environmental harm, and what that means for the choices made at concept stage.

Production

I split the 5.2 kg tyre into its materials using a published passenger-car tyre composition, then applied a raw-material emission factor to each. The raw materials in one tyre come to about 11.3 kg of CO2, and synthetic rubber and carbon black alone make up almost half of it. This covers material production only, not the energy used in the tyre factory or transport.

Use: tyre wear particles

In use, rolling resistance drives energy consumption, and wear releases fine particles that affect air quality. I estimated the particles with the EMEP/EEA emission inventory method:

  • Emission factor: 8.5 mg/km of total suspended particles, the smallest combustion-engine car class, as the powertrain team was using a petrol or hydrogen engine. I carried the published range of 5.3–12.8 mg/km through as the uncertainty.
  • Speed correction: wear per kilometre is highest below 40 km/h (factor 1.39) and falls to 0.90 above 90 km/h.
  • Particle size: 60% of the particles count as PM10 and 42% as PM2.5, particles small enough to travel further and potentially enter the bloodstream.
  • Use case: about 5,000 km a year per vehicle (around 14 km a day), based on the run between Cranfield and Milton Keynes Central station.

End of life and conclusion

Retreading, shredding for material recovery, energy recovery and pyrolysis all reduce the burden, but the literature shows end of life matters far less than production and use. So the biggest levers sit at concept stage: low rolling resistance and the tyre’s materials.

Limits: the material split comes from a larger passenger-car tyre and the emission factors from published studies, not supplier data for this tyre; the production figure covers raw materials only; and end of life was reviewed but not quantified.
