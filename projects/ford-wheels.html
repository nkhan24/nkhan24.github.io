Wheels & Jack Design and Release

Design and release engineer for wheels and jacks at Ford's UK commercial-vehicle engineering centre in Essex, working on Transit programmes and a van developed jointly with Volkswagen, from supplier-led development through to production design release.

What the role involved

  • Design review: checking supplier 3D CAD models and 2D production drawings in CATIA V5 against fit, interface, manufacturing and GD&T requirements, and raising design issues before parts were approved for release.
  • Validation planning: defining and updating DVP&R test requirements and acceptance criteria, assessing technical risk, coordinating validation work and reviewing the test evidence.
  • Physical checks: on prototype vehicles, checking that the jack seats correctly under the jacking point and that there is enough hand clearance to wind the handle past the body side.
  • Problem solving: resolving component issues across design, test and supplier teams, trading off performance, reliability, cost and timing so parts could move through validation to production release.

Example: a jack test failure

Problem: the jack failed validation. Compared with the baseline vehicle, the jacking point had moved and the gross axle weight rating was slightly higher, which changed how force went through the jack as it wound up. The failure was in the heat-affected zone of the weld on the lifting screw, not at the frame's attachment points.

What I did: reviewed the requirements with core engineering, raised a deviation, and logged the concern in WERS, Ford's engineering release system, to track it through to close-out. The jack was strengthened, and its coating was changed to reduce the winding effort.

T = (F·dm / 2) · (l + πμdm) / (πdm − μl)

The torque T needed to raise a load F on a power screw, where dm is the mean thread diameter, l the lead and μ the thread friction coefficient. A lower-friction coating lowers μ, so the same lift needs less torque at the handle.

Result: the jack passed the re-test and was released in line with programme timing.

How a part reaches production release

  1. Supplier design: the supplier designs the wheel or jack to the vehicle's requirements.
  2. CAD and drawing review: I check the models and drawings for fit, interfaces, manufacturability and tolerancing.
  3. DVP&R: the test plan sets what must be proven and the pass criteria for each test.
  4. Validation: parts are built and tested; I review the evidence against the criteria.
  5. Design-verification sign-off: the design is accepted as meeting its requirements.
  6. Production release: the released design goes into production.

Where it connects to the rest of my work

Wheels sit at the point where vehicle requirements turn into a physical part: load rating, interfaces to the hub and brakes, and unsprung mass all meet there. I used the same thinking on the quadricycle wheels and tyres, sizing the tyre from a derived design load and fixing the wheel's PCD early so the suspension design could start.
